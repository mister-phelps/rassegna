# Daily Briefing — Wednesday, 12 August 2026

## EU Affairs

**Von der Leyen prepares a fight over enlargement rules ahead of October summit**
The European Commission is drafting a redesign of the accession rules for new members, to be put to EU leaders at a European Council opening on 15 October, according to three senior officials who spoke to the [Politico Brussels Playbook](https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/brussels-playbook/europes-growing-pains/). The overhaul is meant to answer capitals' fears that decision-making and resource-sharing become harder as the bloc grows, with France, Germany and the Netherlands among those resisting rapid accession — "the fear is we bring in a Trojan horse", one sceptical diplomat told Playbook. Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos confirmed to Politico that Brussels will "soon make proposals" on preparing for a larger union, including firmer rights and responsibilities for new members and safeguards to strip them of the ability to derail decisions. Montenegro hopes to join by 2028, with Ukraine and Moldova behind it, but Hungary has still not cleared the opening of all negotiating clusters for Kyiv and Chișinău even after Viktor Orbán's departure from office. Politico notes that von der Leyen risks presiding over the longest period in the bloc's history without a new member if she cannot unblock the process before leaving office in 2029.

**Commission asks capitals not to enforce its own packaging rules on the day they take effect**
The EU's packaging waste regulation entered into force today, and the Commission is simultaneously urging national authorities not to penalise companies that fail to comply, [Politico](https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/brussels-playbook/europes-growing-pains/) reports. At issue is the requirement that businesses selling across borders appoint an authorised representative in every member state they ship to — proposed in 2022 at the height of the Green Deal, and now judged an unnecessary burden as competitiveness moves up the agenda. Environment Commissioner Jessika Roswall told Playbook that a single EU packaging regime rather than 27 national ones "is good news for businesses" while defending the move to scrap the representative rule, which Parliament and Council have yet to agree. A senior Commission official, granted anonymity, put it bluntly: "There should not be any penalties, any sanctions indeed for anybody, for anything as of tomorrow basically." The European Paper Packaging Alliance told Politico the regulation "has taken up considerable resources" at a time of "challenging economic times and increasing bureaucracy".

**Belgium imported all of its LNG from Russia in July as EU storage runs low**
The whole of the 400,000 tonnes of liquefied natural gas Belgium imported in July came from Russia — the first time Moscow has been the country's sole LNG supplier since 2021, [Le Grand Continent](https://legrandcontinent.eu/fr/2026/08/12/la-russie-a-represente-100-des-importations-de-gnl-de-la-belgique-en-juillet/) reports, drawing on Bloomberg data. Despite the EU's spot-market purchase ban taking effect at the end of April, member states accounted for roughly 45% of Russian LNG offtake last month, ahead of China's 34%, per Le Grand Continent's calculations from CREA figures; cumulative EU purchases of Russian LNG since the invasion now stand at €120bn, above the €110bn spent on Russian oil. The publication attributes the shift to the American-Israeli war against Iran: with Gulf supply disrupted and prices elevated, European buyers deferred orders in the hope of a swift reopening of the Strait of Hormuz. European gas reserves stand at 59.4%, 12.6 points below this time last year, and the EU regulators' agency ACER expects the bloc will need to raise LNG imports before winter. Zeebrugge's outsized regasification capacity — around 9bn cubic metres a year — makes Belgium a redistribution hub for France, the Netherlands, Germany and the UK rather than merely a consumer.

**Europe scrambles for Patriot interceptors before Russia's winter campaign**
European capitals are struggling to find additional Patriot interceptors to donate to Ukraine, which is racing to reinforce its ballistic missile defences ahead of an expected Russian winter assault, according to the [Politico Forecast](https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/forecast/how-friendly-is-too-friendly-with-north-korea/) newsletter. Kyiv is seeking hundreds of interceptors, but new production will arrive too late, the war with Iran has strained US supplies, and several of Ukraine's leading European backers say they have few left that they can safely give away.

## Italian Politics

**Rome's border checks on Spain read as a symptom of Europe fragmenting along national lines**
Italy's decision to impose entry controls on arrivals from Spain — Madrid has retaliated in kind — is the sharpest current illustration of what [Phastidio](https://phastidio.net/2026/08/10/ue-e-immigrazione-finestra-di-overton-spalancata/) calls a Europe in which interdependence has turned from opportunity into problem and, increasingly, threat. Mario Seminerio argues the Italian move is largely performative and electorally motivated, but cautions against the progressive reading that "the right is breaking Europe": in his framing the causation runs the other way, with the right's electoral weight growing because public intolerance of migration is rising. Giorgia Meloni has placed herself at the head of the group of member states that rounded on Spain after the Ceuta arrivals, seeing the episode as an opening to revive her third-country hub project — a "velleitarian forcing", in Seminerio's assessment. He notes that the Return Regulation politically agreed by Parliament and Council on 1 June 2026 creates the first explicit EU legal basis for return hubs, but only after an enforceable return decision exists, which is precisely what distinguishes it from the original Italy–Albania model of processing pending asylum claims offshore. Phastidio's wider warning is that the concept of "safe third country" will keep expanding, that Germany is already pressing Dublin transfers back towards Italy and Greece, and that free movement itself is now at risk.

## UK Politics

**Palantir contracts put Burnham's "buy British" AI pledge to the test**
Prime Minister Andy Burnham's promise to buy British AI faces its first real examination this year, [Politico's](https://www.politico.eu/article/palantir-deals-put-andy-burnham-buy-british-promise-to-the-test/) Joseph Bambridge and Frank Hersey report. The government must decide by year-end whether to renew Palantir's £330mn contract to supply the NHS Federated Data Platform, will award the first contracts under the new Single Patient Record, and is weighing the Home Office's plan to integrate police datasets at an expected £250mn a year — for which Palantir is also in the running. The UK holds more than £600mn in active contracts with the company, which some MPs have called "an unacceptable point of weakness", and pressure has concentrated on Palantir because of its work for the Trump administration and its founders' public remarks. Sceptics warn that distancing the UK from the $300bn firm risks inflaming transatlantic tensions and cutting Britain off from the best available technology.

## Economy & Markets

**US labour market weakens as markets await July inflation data**
American payrolls fell by 23,000 in July and prior months were revised down by 103,000 across May and June, [Le Grand Continent](https://legrandcontinent.eu/fr/2026/08/11/en-un-an-et-demi-trump-a-cree-17-fois-moins-demplois-que-joe-biden/) reports from Labor Department figures. Employment grew just 0.2% year on year — 316,000 posts, roughly half the rate of population growth — and only 590,000 jobs have been created between January 2025 and July 2026, seventeen times fewer than over the equivalent stretch under Joe Biden and well below the 3.4mn added in the first nineteen months of Trump's first term. Unemployment nonetheless fell to 4.1%, a more-than-one-year low, which Le Grand Continent attributes to a shrinking labour force as retirements accelerate and discouraged job-seekers step back; participation is at its lowest since February 2021. Average wage growth is at its slowest since the pandemic began, and inflation outpaced wages in April and May for the first time since May 2023. [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/wall-st-futures-tick-up-heading-into-july-inflation-data-2026-08-12/) reports US stock futures edging higher ahead of today's July inflation print.

**Investors question whether a leaner Fed will pay a price for less guidance**
Markets are testing whether Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh can unwind decades of forward guidance without exacting a cost from financial markets, [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/investors-worry-leaner-fed-guidance-may-come-price-2026-08-12/) reports. The question sits alongside an oil market kept on edge by the deadlocked Iran talks and continuing Red Sea attacks.

**Beijing turns to capital markets to fund its technology race**
China is opening a funding channel it has historically avoided, pushing household savings towards equity and bond issuance to finance its bid for technological leadership, [Phastidio](https://phastidio.net/2026/08/11/capitalisti-di-tutta-la-cina-investite/) argues. Memory-chip maker CXMT rose 550% on its Shanghai STAR Market debut, overtaking ICBC as the most valuable listed company on the mainland, while the retail offer for humanoid robot maker Unitree — listing this month at a $9bn valuation on a price/earnings multiple of 219 — was oversubscribed 5,500 times. Chinese technology firms have raised around $217bn through IPOs and bond sales over two years against more than six dollars raised by US peers for every dollar raised in China, but they borrow at an average coupon of 1.9%, over 300 basis points below American rivals — the widest gap since at least 2015. Seminerio's caution is that channelling the world's largest pool of household savings, some $26tn, into a state-directed technology race carries a real risk of producing a bubble on the model of Chinese property, particularly with the "national team" of state brokers propping up valuations before major listings.

## Foreign Policy

**Fresh attacks on shipping as Iran hardens its terms over Hormuz**
The United States and Yemen's Houthis reported separate attacks on shipping as prospects of ending the Iran war dimmed, with Tehran saying the Strait of Hormuz will stay closed unless Washington accepts its conditions, [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/middle-east/pakistan-says-us-iran-close-some-sort-deal-despite-attacks-shipping-2026-08-11/) reports. [Le Grand Continent](https://legrandcontinent.eu/fr/2026/08/10/liran-affirme-quil-ne-negociera-pas-avec-les-etats-unis-tant-que-donald-trump-sera-president/) sharpens the picture from Iranian sources: Majid Shakeri, an adviser to parliamentary speaker Mohammad Ghalibaf, wrote that Tehran will simply wait Trump out until January 2029, and that "the road to victory is neither fighting nor an agreement, but managing a process that is neither war nor peace" — an approach resting on "denial, ambiguity and strategic patience". Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi has confirmed continuing talks with Oman on reopening the strait while denying direct negotiations with Washington, and the appointment of hardliner Mohsen Rezaee as secretary of the Supreme National Security Council points the same way. Both sides are now demanding reparations: Trump told Axios he is content to let economic pressure build rather than launch a fresh offensive, then posted on Truth Social that the US would seek compensation from Iran for decades of casualties. The two accounts differ in emphasis — Reuters relays Pakistan's claim that Washington and Tehran are close to "some sort of deal", while Le Grand Continent reports an Iranian strategy explicitly designed to avoid one.

**Seoul debates coexistence with a nuclear North as Pyongyang fires again**
North Korea launched a ballistic missile towards the sea off the peninsula's east coast, South Korean and Japanese authorities said, days before major joint exercises by Seoul and Washington, [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/north-korea-fires-unidentified-projectile-media-says-2026-08-11/) reports. The timing lands awkwardly for President Lee Jae-myung, whose Unification Ministry has published a blueprint proposing an initial freeze on North Korea's programme rather than waiting for full denuclearisation, four-party talks to replace the armistice with a permanent peace treaty, revived tourism via the Gyeongwon railway, and dropping the "main enemy" label, [Politico Forecast](https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/forecast/how-friendly-is-too-friendly-with-north-korea/) reports. Foreign Minister Cho Hyun and other lawmakers have criticised the plan as a step back for national defence and "idealistic hope", and Lee has declined to endorse it fully. Washington has already opposed restoring parts of the 2018 inter-Korean military agreement over surveillance concerns, leaving Seoul trying to reopen a channel to a better-armed and more Russia- and China-aligned North without straining its alliance.
