# Daily Briefing — Monday, 3 August 2026

## EU Affairs

**Sánchez stands alone as EU capitals turn on Madrid over Ceuta**
Spain's prime minister has emerged from the mass crossing into Ceuta as, in the words of the [Politico Brussels Playbook](https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/brussels-playbook/the-loneliest-leader-in-the-eu/), "effectively a pariah in the bloc". Some 60,000 people entered the North African exclave on 30–31 July; most had returned to Morocco by Sunday night and none reached the Iberian Peninsula, but [22 leaders signed a letter](https://www.politico.eu/article/ceuta-crisis-22-eu-leaders-letter-spain-pedro-sanchez-migration-policy/) attacking Spain's migration policies as a threat to the bloc's security, with Estonia's Kristen Michal telling Playbook that Madrid's amnesty schemes had created "incentives" for illegal migration. Sánchez replied with a letter of his own calling the bloc's lack of solidarity "selfish". [Politico](https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/brussels-playbook/the-loneliest-leader-in-the-eu/) frames the episode as the culmination of long-building irritation over Spain's low defence spending and its [softer line on China](https://www.politico.eu/article/spain-withdraws-support-eu-china-trade-defenses/), noting that the two other socialist prime ministers at the Council table both signed the letter; [Euractiv](https://www.euractiv.com/) emphasises instead what the row leaves unsaid — that despite reporting Morocco encouraged the crossings, few governments dared point at Rabat, with senior EU officials praising Moroccan authorities for cooperating "very, very fully". The Commission has echoed the criticism, arguing the crisis should be a catalyst for tougher EU migration controls. EU ambassadors met in emergency session at 2:30 p.m. today, with home affairs ministers convening by videoconference on Tuesday.

**The human toll is still rising, and the numbers do not agree**
Figures for the Ceuta crossing have shifted through the day. [Le Grand Continent](https://legrandcontinent.eu/fr/2026/08/02/la-crise-de-ceuta-est-elle-la-premiere-ruee-algorithmique-de-lhistoire/) reported 67 deaths and roughly 48,000 returns as of Sunday; the [Politico Brussels Playbook](https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/brussels-playbook/the-loneliest-leader-in-the-eu/) cited "at least 72 people dead" this morning; by the afternoon [Euractiv's The Brief](https://www.euractiv.com/) put the toll at "at least 88 people". Spanish authorities continue to stress that no one crossed into mainland Spain or the wider Schengen area.

**Was Ceuta the first "algorithmic rush"?**
[Le Grand Continent](https://legrandcontinent.eu/fr/2026/08/02/la-crise-de-ceuta-est-elle-la-premiere-ruee-algorithmique-de-lhistoire/) has assembled what is known about the role of social media in the surge, and the picture is suggestive rather than conclusive. The trigger appears to have been a distorted reading of a 29 June Spanish Supreme Court ruling that summary pushbacks cannot be applied to migrants intercepted swimming — recast online as a guarantee against removal — compounded by a false rumour that the border would "open at 10 p.m." on Wednesday. A Facebook group named *Hraga Ceuta*, spotted by the local press on 27 July and counting more than 20,000 members, circulated Google Maps screenshots of swimming distances around the Tarajal breakwater, photographs of wetsuits and fins, sea conditions and taxi routes; it became inaccessible after the crisis. Le Grand Continent notes that no platform has responded publicly and the Commission has taken no action under the Digital Services Act, with Home Affairs Commissioner Magnus Brunner's response confined to borders, Frontex support and pressure on Rabat. It also sets the episode against structural pressure — youth unemployment in Morocco at 37.2% in 2025, above 45% in urban areas — and against Washington's recent interest in the exclaves: a House Appropriations report in late April described Ceuta and Melilla as "situated in Moroccan territory", and Donald Trump used the crisis on Fox News to warn that "this will be us in three years if the wrong side wins".

**Ombudswoman warns of "accountability-free zones" in Brussels**
European Ombudswoman Teresa Anjinho told the [Politico Brussels Playbook](https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/brussels-playbook/the-loneliest-leader-in-the-eu/) that as the Commission takes on more powers in response to successive crises it is also exercising "more discretion" about what it tells the public, fuelling a "huge surge of complaints" to her office. She called the EU's access-to-documents rules a sign of "structural dysfunction" unfit "for the 21st century" — more than a third of her caseload concerns transparency — and said she is pushing for the regulation to be overhauled to "penalise refusal". "Urgency is not a catch-all justification," she argued. "We cannot … have accountability-free zones." Anjinho did not name Ursula von der Leyen but described the executive's "opacity" as a threat to the European project itself.

**Commission moves to nominate "eminent persons" in possible AfD party ban**

## UK Politics

**Burnham promises metro mayors a share of income tax**
Prime Minister Andy Burnham has announced what the government calls the largest transfer of powers and resources from Westminster in a generation: from 2028, English combined authorities led by metro mayors — Greater Manchester, the West Midlands, Liverpool City Region, West Yorkshire, South Yorkshire and others — will retain a share of the income tax collected in their territory, with a larger share of business rates retained from 2027 and a tourist tax to follow. [Phastidio](https://phastidio.net/2026/08/03/burnham-e-il-fisco-di-prossimita/) stresses that this is not a new local levy nor a power to set rates: a portion of revenue that currently flows entirely to the Treasury will simply stay where it was generated, to be spent on transport, housing, urban regeneration and infrastructure. The exact percentage will be set in the autumn budget. Mario Seminerio's verdict is sceptical: buoyant areas such as London, Greater Manchester and Cambridgeshire will generate far more than weaker tax bases, the promised equalisation mechanisms remain undefined, tying local budgets to income tax makes them pro-cyclical and creates an incentive to court high earners over poorer households, and English councils have lost much of the planning and financial capacity that fiscal autonomy demands. Conservative mayors have already said they would return their share to taxpayers as rebates — not what Labour has in mind. Phastidio reads the whole exercise as further evidence of Britain's "Italian" trajectory, where devolution debates ended in a net rise in the overall tax burden: "the road to hell is paved with good devolutions".

## Economy & Markets

**Washington intervenes to prop up the yen — and sells euros to do it**
On 31 July the US Treasury bought yen for the first time since 1998, in what [Le Grand Continent](https://legrandcontinent.eu/fr/2026/08/03/la-doctrine-bessent-fait-du-dollar-la-monnaie-de-la-vassalisation-heureuse/) calls the largest coordinated currency operation since 2011. The sequence began on 30 July, with the yen at 163 to the dollar — its weakest since 1986 — and Tokyo mounting the biggest single-session intervention in its history, estimated at $53–59bn, alongside a parallel move by the Bank of Korea. The New York Fed then bought yen on the Treasury's behalf not against dollars but against euros, an operation HSBC analysts called "highly unusual, maybe unprecedented"; the ECB, not consulted, has declined to comment. Both governments confirmed the action today, with Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama citing coordination "with the Treasury Department" and Scott Bessent promising not to hesitate over further joint interventions to correct "the substantial undervaluation of the yen". Shahin Vallée reads the episode as the Miran doctrine applied to the letter — discretionary monetary protection extended to the most aligned allies, priced against the $550bn of Japanese and $350bn of Korean investment commitments made under the 2025 trade deals, and costing Washington nothing. Trump supplied the political register: it is "a signal of friendship … Japan's been very good to us, with the exception, of course, of Pearl Harbor." The question Vallée leaves for Europe is what a currency is worth when it has neither a protector nor a doctrine of its own.

**Williams says the Fed will raise rates if inflation does not ease**
New York Fed President John Williams said he remains optimistic that inflation pressures are on track to ease gradually, but that if they do not the central bank "will not hesitate to respond with rate hikes" to return price pressures to target, [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/) reports. The remarks land against a weakening backdrop: US factories faced softer demand and higher costs in July as the Iran war ground on, while the war-driven oil rally is set to lift shale profits to their highest in years.

**Europe's rivers hit record lows, with industrial and political consequences**
The Rhine is forecast to reach its lowest level ever measured at the Kaub gauge near Koblenz around Tuesday 4 August — 26cm on 31 July, falling to 24cm — according to [Le Grand Continent](https://legrandcontinent.eu/fr/2026/08/02/la-secheresse-a-fait-baisser-le-niveau-des-fleuves-europeens-a-leur-plus-bas-niveau/), citing the German federal waterways administration. Navigation becomes impossible below 40cm; barge freight from Rotterdam to points south of Kaub has jumped to €145 per tonne, five times the €29 charged this time last year. The Kiel Institute estimates the effect at 0.1–0.2% of German GDP in the third quarter. The Danube fell to 1,600 cubic metres per second at the Romanian border on 29 July, roughly a third of its July average, and the consequences are already political: Hungary has been forced to shut the Paks nuclear plant, which supplies 40% of its electricity, for the first time in 44 years. [Politico](https://www.politico.eu/article/hungary-shuts-down-paks-nuclear-plant-danube-drought-intensifies-energy-crisis/) reports Péter Magyar describing a "critical" week ahead; [Euractiv](https://www.euractiv.com/) adds that Budapest has asked households to avoid using washing machines and dryers at peak times and has roughly doubled electricity imports, most of the additional power coming from Czechia.

## Foreign Policy

**Trump calls off strikes; Tehran denies talks are under way**
Donald Trump said the United States would hold fresh talks with Iran today after cancelling planned strikes over the weekend, but Iran says no negotiations are taking place, [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/) reports — leaving the president's stated justification for standing down without Iranian confirmation. Reuters' [analysis](https://www.reuters.com/world/middle-east/how-iran-is-widening-its-pressure-campaign-force-us-concessions-2026-08-03/) argues Tehran is betting it can outlast Washington by widening its pressure campaign, turning the region's trade routes, shipping lanes and energy infrastructure into pressure points that steadily raise the cost of confrontation — a strategy Gulf officials and analysts describe as treating disruption of trade as Iran's strongest asset. Oil fell on the news that the attack had been called off. Separately, Israeli strikes killed 18 people in Gaza, with an Israeli minister saying no deal to halt the fighting is in place.

**Ukrainian strikes on refineries push Russia into importing petrol**
Repeated Ukrainian attacks on refineries across Russian territory have produced a severe fuel shortage, [Le Grand Continent](https://legrandcontinent.eu/fr/2026/08/03/economie-russe-la-crise-des-carburants-sapprofondit-et-les-exportations-chutent/) reports. Official Russian figures show Super 95 rising from $0.55 a litre in December 2024 to $0.93 in July 2026 — a jump of nearly 70% in eighteen months — after which the government barred Rosstat from publishing its weekly price data. Queues and rationing have spread, notably in Crimea, and Russia, once among the world's largest exporters of refined products, has been importing petrol from India and Kazakhstan. Export bans on petrol and diesel, due to lapse at the end of July, were extended on 30 July to 31 January 2027. CREA data show exports of petroleum and chemical products falling from more than 400,000 tonnes a day in early 2022 to 150,000 now, though crude exports have held up, helped by increased use of the Arctic route.
