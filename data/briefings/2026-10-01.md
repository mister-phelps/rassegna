# Daily Briefing — Thursday, 1 October 2026

## EU Affairs

**The Commission tightens its grip on the EU's purse strings**
Officials from Ursula von der Leyen's Secretariat-General convene government experts in Brussels today to discuss "examples of reforms, investments and outputs" and the "regional and territorial aspects" of roughly half the EU's approximately €2 trillion next seven-year budget, according to an agenda obtained by the [Politico Brussels Playbook](https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/brussels-playbook/the-fight-to-control-the-eus-purse-strings/). Politico frames the meeting as a window onto how far budget power is migrating into von der Leyen's inner circle: the session is opened by Declan Costello of SG REFORM, the task force that helped steer the post-Covid recovery fund — the cash-for-reforms template for the Commission's proposed National and Regional Partnership Plans, which would bundle agricultural, regional and migration spending into national pots. Ten member states from Poland to Luxembourg have already objected to making payments conditional on reforms, fearing Brussels could demand politically explosive changes to pensions or the judiciary. "We did and will today again make clear that there is no room for diluting the regional dimension," a Polish official told Playbook. The directorates-general that traditionally held the spending power — AGRI, REGIO, HOME, EMPL and MARE — attend as bit-part players, several of them facing possible mergers in a forthcoming Commission reshuffle.

**Enlargement: Brussels dangles single-market access while Berlin pushes for a Montenegrin deadline**
The Commission will offer candidate countries phased, tariff-free access to the single market provided they align with the bloc against hostile states and industrial rivals, according to a draft proposal obtained by [Politico](https://www.politico.eu/article/ursula-von-der-leyen-eu-offer-single-market-access-to-candidate-countries/). Reporting from Podgorica, where EU and Western Balkan foreign ministers met this morning under the twelve-year-old Berlin Process, [Politico's Berlin Playbook](https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/berlin-playbook/) presents the offer more sceptically — as "a kind of reward while real EU accession keeps them waiting." German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul told the newsletter that "whoever makes progress on reforms must also make progress in the accession process," arguing Montenegro's accession treaty should be concluded by the end of this year or early 2027, before the French presidential election, even if ratification follows the vote. Montenegro, a candidate for nearly sixteen years, wants accession in 2028; Berlin opposes "coupling" frontrunners to slower applicants. Wadephul's warning: "Only if we offer the Western Balkan states a credible EU perspective and bind them more closely to us do we secure lasting geopolitical room for manoeuvre."

**Romania's political crisis deepens as third prime ministerial nominee falls**
Parliament rejected MEP Siegfried Mureșan, President Nicușor Dan's third choice for prime minister, in an afternoon vote on Wednesday. [Politico](https://www.politico.eu/article/romanian-political-crisis-deepens-as-parliament-rejects-pm-nominee-siegfried-muresan/) reports the result "brings the country a step closer to a snap election that could hand power to the far right," and notes the practical consequence for Brussels: the EU's budget negotiator returns to his day job at a critical moment in the MFF talks. **The Commission's "sovereign" alternative to Teams gets brutal reviews**
Element Pro, the communications tool the Commission rolled out over the summer and billed in a staff memo as a "sovereign back-up to Teams," has been ridiculed by officials who told [Politico](https://www.politico.eu/article/eu-microsoft-teams-alternative-bad-reviews-element-pro/) the software is "absolute shit." One said that, judging by how well it works, the United States would "instantly win the war" if it ever switched off its technology. The Commission told staff in July that "in recent months, digital sovereignty has become increasingly important," meaning "greater control over our data and systems so that we can reduce risks and limit disruption." Element's chief executive Matthew Hodgson responded that "change is always painful, particularly if it's" a switch from a programme as embedded as Teams. The rollout sits alongside France's decision to move millions of civil servants off US video tools by 2027 and Austria's military switch from Microsoft Office to LibreOffice.

## Italian Politics

**Meloni asks Brussels to loosen the fiscal rules over energy costs**
Giorgia Meloni will ask von der Leyen to relax the Commission's fiscal rules so governments can help companies and households absorb energy-driven price rises, and wants the question taken up at the coming Ecofin and European Council meetings, [Politico](https://www.politico.eu/article/giorgia-meloni-fiscal-rules-spending-plea-energy-crunch-ursula-von-der-leyen/) reports. The plea places Rome alongside Athens, which is making a parallel case for exceptional flexibility. [Phastidio](https://phastidio.net/2026/09/23/la-breccia-a-via-venti-settembre/) has argued the harder constraint is structural: Italy remains in the excessive deficit procedure and cannot net off the additional flexibility it is requesting, so any extra deficit stays an extra deficit whether or not it is excluded from European statistics.

**The budget season opens with more flat taxes, not fewer tax breaks**
Italy's tax expenditures are worth some €110 billion a year across 625 separate items, and deputy finance minister Maurizio Leo — interviewed in *Corriere della Sera* — proposes convening "a table with all the parties, including the opposition, and the social partners" to thin them out, because "every political force fears losing votes." [Phastidio](https://phastidio.net/2026/09/29/troppe-tax-expenditures-ne-servono-altre/) reads the interview as self-refuting: in the same breath Leo defends an expanding family of flat taxes — 12.5% on government bond interest, 26% on financial income, a 21% flat rate on rents, 5% on collectively bargained pay rises, 1% on productivity bonuses, plus special rates for night work, tourism and doctors' overtime — and floats two new ones, a 5% rate on year-on-year pay rises for workers under 35 and an extension of the *cedolare secca* to shop leases. Leo concedes this "may seem to contradict the principle of progressivity enshrined in the formal constitution," but argues "there is then a material constitution that must accommodate real situations and the needs of the community." Mario Seminerio's verdict is that the proliferation of selective flat taxes is hollowing out income tax and loading the cost onto the salaried taxpayers who cannot escape it: "this is a country that is devouring itself."

**The contradiction at the heart of the "Made in EU" row**
[Phastidio](https://phastidio.net/2026/09/28/siamo-sovrani-fateci-entrare/) sets Burnham's request that the UK be treated as a "trusted partner" inside the perimeter of the Commission's Industrial Accelerator Act against the domestic pressure on him to adopt a "Buy British" approach to the roughly £400 billion a year the British public sector spends on procurement. UK–EU automotive trade alone is worth around €80 billion a year with supply chains crossing the Channel continuously, so excluding British components would hurt continental manufacturers too; from this month, British public projects above £10 million or using at least 500 tonnes of steel must record whether the steel was UK-made and, if not, why. Seminerio notes that France wants the "Made in EU" perimeter kept close to the single market while Germany, with roughly $52 billion of goods trade with Turkey in 2025 and Turkish plants embedded in its car supply chains, prefers "Made with EU" and wants Ankara included — raising the awkward question of "why Ankara and not London?" **France: "nobody seems to have grasped the danger zone we are entering"**
The OAT-Bund spread has moved from under 70 basis points in the spring to around 85 in late August and now above 110, meaning France borrows 1.1% more dearly than Germany, Shahin Vallée — formerly Macron's economic adviser at Bercy — told [Le Grand Continent](https://legrandcontinent.eu/fr/2026/09/30/la-dette-francaise-est-en-train-de-decrocher/). He estimates the three-week move has added the equivalent of €15 billion a year in debt service over a decade, close to €100 billion cumulatively — roughly one and a half times the justice budget, against total French public investment of about €130 billion a year. France is now walking towards its euro-era record of 211 basis points, set in November 2011; the spread stayed under 150 even during the 1992 franc crisis. Vallée's warning is about reflexivity: once the interest rate exceeds nominal growth, the debt ratio rises mechanically and higher rates beget further market anxiety. "Nobody seems to want to talk, in the press or in the political class, about this loss of control of the OAT-Bund spread."

**Lecornu's budget lands — and his own ministries say they were kept in the dark**
France's 2027 finance and social security bills were printed overnight — some 400,000 pages — and are presented to the press this morning ahead of the Council of Ministers, [Politico's Playbook Paris](https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/playbook-paris/) reports. Its scoop is that ministers, cabinets and administrations on the front line of the process felt kept at arm's length by Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu: officials at the Public Accounts ministry "learned of the €54 billion savings figure on the very day the Prime Minister announced it" in *Le Figaro*, and had to reverse-engineer within hours how Matignon had calculated it. The known arbitrages: a €54 billion effort weighted towards spending, a cut in the surtax on large companies, and €5.5 billion from pensioners via a lower ceiling on the 10% professional-expenses allowance and de-indexation of pensions above €1,260. Labour Minister Jean-Pierre Farandou told *Challenges* the aim is to [roughly halve the social security deficit in 2027](https://www.challenges.fr/economie/jean-pierre-farandou-nous-voulons-diviser-quasiment-par-deux-le-deficit-de-la-securite-sociale-en-2027_646080).

**Passengers overpower a co-pilot who tried to bring down a Tel Aviv flight**
Passengers on flydubai flight FZ1073 from Dubai stormed the cockpit and restrained the first officer after he stabbed the captain and, Israeli officials say, apparently attempted to crash the aircraft with more than 170 people aboard. [The Times](https://www.thetimes.com/world/middle-east/article/israel-plane-attack-nnzqlvn5h) carries the fullest reconstruction: the first officer, an Omani citizen, seized control over Saudi Arabia two hours into the flight; the seriously wounded captain, Smit Machchhar, an Indian national, managed to press the release switch on the secure cockpit door, letting passengers force their way in; two off-duty company pilots travelling in the cabin took the controls to pull the aircraft out of its dive, and at 9.31am Dubai time someone on the flight deck set the transponder to 7700. **Russia's "war of the cities": the doctrine behind the strikes on Ukraine**
The UN human rights mission in Ukraine recorded at least 200 civilians killed and 1,234 injured between 1 and 24 September, with attacks on civilians in Kyiv on 16 of those 24 days — a frequency "never recorded in the capital since the start of the full-scale invasion" — according to [Le Grand Continent](https://legrandcontinent.eu/fr/2026/10/01/la-guerre-des-villes-la-doctrine-russe-pour-devaster-lukraine/). Over January to August the toll reached 2,222 killed and 13,058 injured, 55% above the same period in 2025 and more than double 2024, with long-range weapons responsible for 47% of casualties; Zelenskyy said Russia launched more than 2,200 drones, 1,630 glide bombs and 38 missiles in a single week. The analysis is notable for taking the Russian military Telegram channel Rybar at its word: the "war of the cities" is presented there as a strategy of economic attrition designed to turn Ukraine into "a useless asset for its sponsors," costing Europe more than €50 billion a year while unable to sustain itself, until the Union comes to "consider the country lost." The escalation coincides with a stalled front — Russia took just 5 km² in August, its slowest advance since October 2023 — and with signs of domestic strain: the Russian institute IKAR finds 81% of Russians would want the war to end "tomorrow," a record.
