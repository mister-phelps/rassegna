# Daily Briefing — Thursday, 13 August 2026

## EU Affairs

**EU heads for another inconclusive clash over Israel sanctions**
Member states are set to return to the question of sanctions against Israel at an informal foreign ministers' meeting (a "Gymnich") in Wicklow, Ireland, on 1–2 September, but three European diplomats involved in the negotiations told the [Politico Brussels Playbook](https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/brussels-playbook/the-eus-middle-east-impasse/) that agreement remains unlikely. Kaja Kallas, who is convening the meeting and will circulate the agenda in the coming days, has spent the past year trying to assemble the qualified majority needed to suspend trade with Israeli settlements in the West Bank and the unanimity required to blacklist far-right ministers. Israel's rejection of the 15-point Gaza plan drawn up by Donald Trump's Board of Peace — Benjamin Netanyahu [refused the proposed military withdrawal](https://www.politico.eu/article/israel-rejects-us-backed-gaza-peace-plan-hamas-disarmament/) ahead of full Hamas disarmament — has removed the argument, advanced by Czechia and Germany among others, that action would disrupt live peace talks. But diplomats say a fresh objection has taken its place: with Israel's election due on 27 October, sanctions on figures such as National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir could backfire and lift his Otzma Yehudit party in the polls. Israel's ambassador to the bloc, Avi Nir-Feldklein, told Playbook that "anyone that is concerned about the Palestinians, the answer is to work with Israel, not against us."

**Washington is content to wait out Netanyahu's rejection**
[Politico's Forecast newsletter](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/08/12/tolerating-netanyahu-two-step-why-white-house-can-live-with-israels-rejection-peace-plan-01033659) reports that the Trump administration can live with the Israeli refusal for now, on the reasoning that it is less concerned with what Netanyahu says than with what the Israel Defense Forces do. "We recognize the complexities that come from the Israeli elections, and we're obviously going to give [Netanyahu] a space to try and navigate that," an administration official said. The framing differs from Brussels: [Politico](https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/brussels-playbook/the-eus-middle-east-impasse/) presents the rejection as removing an obstacle to EU action, while the White House reading treats it as electoral theatre that changes little on the ground. Hildegard Bentele, the German MEP who chairs the European Parliament's delegation to Israel, told Playbook she had expected the rejection, since anything short of insisting on Hamas' disarmament is "very hard to explain to an Israeli public, whose main concerns are still security concerns."

**Breton: the Digital Omnibus has hollowed out the AI Act at the moment of its entry into force**
Writing in [Le Grand Continent](https://legrandcontinent.eu/fr/2026/08/13/pourquoi-nous-navons-pas-encore-bien-compris-lai-act/), Thierry Breton — one of the regulation's principal architects — argues that the AI Act's core has been displaced just as it takes effect. Article 50, on transparency for chatbots, deepfakes and synthetic content, entered into application on 2 August 2026 and now dominates public debate; but the Council's adoption on 29 June 2026 of the "Digital Omnibus on AI" pushed the Annex III high-risk obligations back to 2 December 2027 and those under Annex I to 2 August 2028. Breton calls the delay "a climbdown" secured by a coalition of American lobbies, some European chief executives and several capitals including Paris, and warns that leaving a labelling requirement as the regulation's only visible face reduces "a regulation of civilisation to a regulation of marking". He also notes that statistical watermarking of text is technically circumventable, and that the deepfake and high-risk provisions — not detection tools — were the point. The text was adopted by the European Parliament on 13 March 2024 by 523 votes to 46 with 49 abstentions, and unanimously by the Council on 21 May 2024.

The measure is framed as a response to mounting threats from Russia and marks a significant step in long-running plans to strengthen German intelligence capabilities.

## Italian Politics

**Rome's border checks on Spain and the widening Overton window on migration**
Mario Seminerio argues in [Phastidio](https://phastidio.net/2026/08/10/ue-e-immigrazione-finestra-di-overton-spalancata/) that the Italy–Spain quarrel over Schengen — Madrid imposed retaliatory border controls on Italy on 7 August — is a symptom of a European order in which interdependence has turned from opportunity into threat, across migration, tax, industrial policy, state aid and financial markets. He reads the coalition of EU governments of varying political colours that lined up against Spain after the Ceuta crossings as a conditioned reflex of national interest, and describes Italy's own entry controls as performative and electorally motivated. Giorgia Meloni, on this account, placed herself at the head of that group in order to relaunch her third-country hub project — a "wishful overreach", in Seminerio's view, because the Return Regulation politically agreed by Parliament and Council on 1 June 2026 permits return hubs only where an enforceable return decision already exists, and not as a venue for processing pending asylum claims. That is precisely what distinguishes the EU instrument from the original Italian centres in Albania. He notes that Germany's CDU has carried the same position as the Meloni government in its programme for two years, and that Berlin has now opened moves to revive Dublin transfers towards Italy and Greece.

## UK Politics

**Farage cruises towards re-election in Clacton, but the by-election has cost him goodwill**
Polls opened on Thursday in Clacton, where Nigel Farage faces 33 rival candidates on what [Politico's Forecast newsletter](https://www.politico.eu/article/nigel-farage-vs-count-binface-inside-the-clacton-by-election-circus/) calls the longest ballot paper in British history, his most prominent challenger being the satirical candidate Count Binface. Constituency polling cited by Politico puts Farage on 73% to Binface's 20%, but two focus groups the outlet attended found a recurring complaint that the contest has made the town "a laughing stock" — a charge directed at Farage rather than at his comic opponents. The by-election is self-inflicted: Farage resigned his seat amid two parliamentary investigations into undeclared donations, one concerning £5 million from a crypto-entrepreneur and another into support from a convicted fraudster, denying wrongdoing in both and arguing the help was given in a personal capacity. [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/uk/reform-uks-farage-faces-off-against-count-binface-election-2026-08-13/) frames the vote more narrowly as a test of opposition to Reform UK's brand of populism, while Politico emphasises the reputational cost and the risk that a standards ruling forces a second, far harder contest. The mainstream parties declined to stand.

## Economy & Markets

**Tokyo and Washington intervene jointly in the yen for the first time since 1998**
Japan spent $87 billion buying yen in an attempt to arrest the currency's slide, with the United States also contributing — the first joint intervention in the yen market by the two countries since the Asian financial crisis of 1998, according to figures cited by [Politico's Forecast newsletter](https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/forecast/inside-the-uks-farage-vs-binface-circus/) from the Peterson Institute. [Phastidio](https://phastidio.net/2026/08/04/bessent-cerotti-allo-yen-stampelle-al-treasury/) reads the operation less as currency management than as Treasury protection: Seminerio's argument is that Scott Bessent's plausible objective is to forestall Japanese liquidation of US Treasuries at the worst possible moment for America, while the yen's underlying weakness reflects fundamentals that intervention does not address.

**European demand keeps global EV sales rising as China and North America soften**

## Foreign Policy

**The air war over Ukraine and Russia is killing civilians at an accelerating rate**
Nearly 9,400 civilians were killed or injured in Ukraine in the first six months of 2026, 37% more than in the same period of 2025 and roughly double the 4,382 recorded in the first half of 2024, [Le Grand Continent](https://legrandcontinent.eu/fr/2026/08/13/en-ukraine-et-en-russie-la-multiplication-des-attaques-aeriennes-alourdit-le-bilan-civil/) reports on UN human rights office figures; June was the deadliest month for Ukrainian civilians since April 2022, with at least 293 killed and 1,990 wounded. Russia fired 376 missiles at Ukraine in July, the highest monthly total of the war and a 90% year-on-year increase, and drone launches approached 10,000 in May against roughly 500 a month at the same point in 2024. The toll inside Russia is rising too: the Conflict Intelligence Team counts 327 civilians killed by Ukrainian strikes this year, twice the 145 recorded in 2025, with drones responsible for 86% of Russian civilian casualties — though, as the piece is careful to note, Ukraine does not deliberately target Russian residential buildings, and its recent campaigns have struck refineries and warehouses close to urban centres. Russia's foreign ministry claims close to 800 civilian deaths since January.

**Grain becomes an instrument of the war**
Russia and Ukraine are now striking each other's grain shipments as a way of gaining advantage away from a largely frozen front line, raising fears of disruption to global supply and prices, [Reuters reports](https://www.reuters.com/world/ukraines-farmers-despair-russias-black-sea-blockade-traps-their-harvest-2026-08-13/). [Politico](https://www.politico.eu/article/ukraine-russia-war-grain-europe-protectionism-drought/) frames the same story through the politics of the European market, arguing that Kyiv's agricultural exports risk being cut off both by Russia's Black Sea attacks and by hesitancy among Ukraine's EU allies. Ukraine's overnight bombardment of Russia's Krasnodar region killed two people and injured at least twelve, [Politico reports](https://www.politico.eu/article/russia-krasnodar-novorossiysk-ukraine-war-attack/); Novorossiysk mayor Andrei Kravchenko said one child was among the dead, while Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Kyiv had targeted the naval base there, calling it "the last major stronghold of the Russian fleet in the Black Sea."

**Iran says talks on reviving the interim deal have stalled**
A senior Iranian source told [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/iran-says-no-progress-reviving-interim-peace-deal-with-us-2026-08-12/) there has been no progress in negotiations to revive the interim agreement reached in June or to set a timetable for implementing it, leaving Tehran and Washington at loggerheads over a permanent end to the war in the Gulf. The Reuters Daily Briefing links the impasse to a further Trump broadside against Iran's leadership and to renewed attacks on shipping in the region. The stalemate is consistent with the position [Le Grand Continent](https://legrandcontinent.eu/fr/2026/08/10/liran-affirme-quil-ne-negociera-pas-avec-les-etats-unis-tant-que-donald-trump-sera-president/) reported on 10 August, in which Tehran's strategy is described explicitly as managing "a process that is neither war nor peace."

**Questions over who is attacking fishing boats off Latin America**
[Le Grand Continent](https://legrandcontinent.eu/fr/2026/08/13/ladministration-trump-a-t-elle-recours-a-des-corsaires-au-large-de-lamerique-latine/) examines evidence that the Trump administration may have authorised letters of marque allowing private operators to hunt and destroy vessels. Since Operation "Southern Spear" began in September, at least 66 strikes have been carried out against boats suspected of drug trafficking, killing at least 221 people; CSIS put the operation's cost at $31 million a day in January. The Ecuadorian fishing vessel *Fiorella* disappeared in the Pacific in late January with eight of its ten crew still missing, and survivors described English-speaking operators wearing US flags but no unit insignia (additional context via the *New York Times*, cited by Le Grand Continent). An aircraft that overflew the boat was registered not to the Pentagon but to a company domiciled at a UPS centre in Richmond, Virginia. The Pentagon declines to confirm or deny whether letters of marque have been issued; a bill authorising them, introduced by Senator Mike Lee in December, has never been voted on.
